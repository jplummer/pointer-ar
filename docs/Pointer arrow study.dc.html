Pointer

arrow geometry study

Pointer overlays a single arrow on the live camera and rotates it toward a target — a landmark, a satellite, a bearing. The arrow has one job, say which way, and two hard cases: when it points straight at you, and straight away. What follows is four interpretations of that brief, each a vector drawn as a real object rather than a flat glyph, and each engineered so the two hard cases fall out of the geometry on their own.

01 — NOTATION

The two degenerate views

Seen end-on, an arrow loses its length and collapses to its cross-section. Pointer turns that liability into the readout. Aimed at the camera, the tip resolves to a point inside its own circular section — the engineer's ⊙, a vector out of the page. Aimed away, the tail resolves to a cross inside that same circle — ⊗, a vector into the page. Neither symbol is drawn. The cone makes the dot — its point tipped in a single accent color so “toward” reads at a glance — and the cruciform tail makes the cross.

Both plates above are the same arrow, photographed down its own axis. No special-case rendering — only the model, rotated.

02 — SHADING & LEGIBILITY

Constant, and never of the scene

One matte material, lit from a fixed point in the arrow's own frame. Scene light never reaches it, so it looks identical at noon and at midnight and never passes for a thing in the photograph. Because the light is pinned to the arrow, the shading stays constant — a smooth bright-to-dark roll from top to underside — which is how elevation reads at a glance, with a single accent tip marking the nose.

The surface is smooth and faintly glossy — enough modeling to read as a solid, machined object rather than a flat glyph — while the even matte finish and hard accent keep it unmistakably synthetic against any photograph.

· body — smooth matte, view-independent
· accent — one warm tone at nose & tail
· no cast shadow, no depth-of-field blur

Elevation is never ambiguous: the shaft tilts out of the horizon plane and the lit top face turns toward or away from you.

04 — FOUR GEOMETRIES

Four readings of one vector

Each is shown as four constant-shaded plates — side, end-on toward, end-on away, and three-quarter — at a shared scale. Dimensions are in scene units (the live model is normalized to roughly the same overall length).

A · Needle

slim, precise, minimum occlusion

The least arrow that still reads. A near-constant 0.12-unit shaft occludes as little of the scene behind it as possible, and a long, narrow cone removes all doubt about which end leads. The tail is four flat cruciform vanes inside a thin nock ring — they do nothing aerodynamic, they exist so that end-on the tail resolves cleanly to ⊗. Choose this when the priority is seeing through the overlay.

B · Spindle

tapered shaft, direction in every section

A shaft that tapers from ⌀0.31 at the nock to ⌀0.12 under the tip. The taper does the work color isn't allowed to: every cross-section is fractionally larger toward the tail, so direction is legible from the side even where both ends are hidden behind UI. The tail is a turned collar with a cross milled into its face — machined, not decorated. The most instrument-like of the four.

C · Fletched dart

aerodynamic — the head draws the circle, no ring

The refinement, and the front-runner. The ring is gone — the head supplies the circle. Seen end-on from behind, the cone base reads as the disk and the four slim vanes read as the cross, so ⊗ resolves with nothing added. The shaft is a slim constant rod — no taper — so it hides as little of the scene as possible, and the small sharp head — its point tipped in accent — turns to a clean ⊙ with a colored dot dead-center when it faces you. Less hardware, more arrow.

D · Banded gauge

the shaft as a measuring instrument

The shaft as scale. Three raised index bands turn the constant-radius rod into a ruler and give the eye fixed marks to read rotation and elevation against — useful parallax when the arrow is nearly end-on. The tail is the flattest of the four: a thin disk with a recessed cross, the quietest possible ⊗. Reads most like a piece of survey equipment, which suits the compass-bezel scene below.

05 — ASSEMBLY

Arrow inside the bezel

The arrow's center sits at the center of a translucent horizon disk — a faint membrane carried all the way to the middle, so the shaft visibly pierces the plane rather than floating above a hole. Degree ticks ring the bezel; the target bearing is a plain double tick at 0°, no accent fill. Disk and arrow move as one rigid unit, the arrow pivoting about the disk center, so its tail dips below the horizon as the tip rises. Shown with the refined dart.

06 — GROUND PLANE · SPINNER

The waiting state, in plane

Before a fix resolves there is no arrow and no bearing — only a sign the instrument is live. The spinner is the compass bezel stripped to its outer ring: same plane, same scale, thin and white, lying flat on the ground rather than turning in screen space. A ring spun in its own plane shows no motion, so the life comes from a single crisp highlight traveling the rim. No ticks, no fill, no center mark — nothing that could pass for the finished compass. Five readings, shown at the shallow tilt of a phone held up to the scene.

Two speeds, one motif

Speed carries meaning. Finding position turns once a second; loading orbit data turns half as fast — slow reads as “waiting on the network, not the sensors.” Shown on the sweep — the motif carried into the bezel and transition.

This became that

The wait is open-ended — a fix can land in half a second or after a minute — so the spinner free-runs at 1 Hz the whole time. When the fix lands it simply winds down where it is: the highlight decelerates to a stop, the rim sprouts its degree ticks — among them the fixed 0° double-tick at the scale zero and a caret at the target azimuth — the horizon membrane fades in, and the dart fades in through the disk center, pointing to that caret. There’s no mark to hit and nothing to reload — the spinner just becomes the bezel. (The demo varies the search time each pass, so it stops in a different place every loop.)

07 — PROFILES

Silhouettes, one scale

Stripped to outline, the four read as a family: same length, same job, four different attitudes toward the tail.

All four hit the brief: crisply synthetic, unambiguous in azimuth and elevation, and self-resolving into ⊙ and ⊗ at the poles with no special-case code. The refined dart (C) is the pick — a slim untapered shaft, a small sharp head that doubles as the ⊗ circle, four slim vanes, and no ring.
